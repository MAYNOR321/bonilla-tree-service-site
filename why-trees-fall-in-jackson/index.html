Why trees in Jackson fall over whole — and which ones to watch

By Bonilla Tree Service crew · Published August 31, 2026 · Updated August 31, 2026

Trees in the Jackson metro tend to fall over whole — root ball and all — rather than snapping, and the reason is under the ground, not in the tree. Most of Hinds and Madison counties sit on Yazoo clay, an expansive soil that swells when it's wet and shrinks when it dries. Over the years that movement works a tree's root plate loose. Then a storm arrives on ground that several days of rain have already saturated, the wind loads the canopy, and the whole tree tips instead of breaking. That's why so many storm calls in Jackson, Madison, Ridgeland and Pearl are for pines lying flat with the roots in the air. This page explains what Yazoo clay is, which trees fail most often here, when they fail, and what to look for in your own yard before the next storm.

What is Yazoo clay?

Yazoo clay is a mineral-rich expansive clay that runs in a band roughly 40 miles wide and 120 miles long across central Mississippi, from Yazoo County to Scott County. It underlies eleven counties, including Hinds and Madison — which means most of Jackson, Ridgeland, Madison and Clinton sit on it. What makes it a problem is how much it moves: it absorbs water and swells, then dries and shrinks. Published engineering studies have measured swelling of more than 200 percent and pressures above 25,000 pounds per square foot. Industry estimates put the share of metro Jackson homes with some degree of clay-related damage at up to 65 percent. Foundations are the usual headline. But trees live in the same soil, and every wet-and-dry cycle moves the ground they are anchored in.

Why does the clay make a tree fall over instead of breaking?

A healthy tree on stable ground usually fails at a weak point — a rotten limb, a split fork — and the trunk stays standing. On Yazoo clay the weak point is the anchorage. As the clay swells and shrinks through the seasons, the soil around the root plate loosens a little at a time. Nothing looks wrong from the yard. Then a storm system arrives after several days of rain. The clay is saturated and soft, the canopy is heavy with water, and the wind pushes on all of it at once. The roots have nothing firm to hold, so the tree rotates out of the ground and comes down whole, taking a slab of turf with it. That is the failure we see most on storm calls across the metro, and it is the most dangerous kind, because the entire weight of the tree lands at once.

Which trees in the Jackson metro fail most often?

From our own removals across Jackson, Madison, Ridgeland, Brandon, Pearl, Clinton and Flowood since 2022, three species account for most of the storm work:

  • Loblolly pine — the most common tree in the area and the one we remove most after storms. It grows fast and tall, 80 to 90 feet is normal, on a root plate that stays wide and shallow. A tall, heavy top on a shallow anchor in moving clay is built to tip.
  • Water oak — grows quickly to a large size but is short-lived for an oak, and it decays from the inside. A water oak can look full and green while the trunk is hollow. Older neighborhoods in Clinton and Jackson have many that are past their safe age.
  • Sweetgum — less likely to fall whole, but it sheds large limbs and drops spiked seed pods all winter. Usually a trimming job rather than a removal.

If you only check one thing: pines within falling distance of the house.

When do trees fall in Jackson?

The pattern is consistent: not the first day of a storm, but a wind event that arrives on already-wet ground. Three or four days of rain saturate the clay, then a front comes through with gusts, and that's when the calls start. Spring and early summer storm season is the peak, but a winter ice storm on soaked ground does the same thing. Near the Ross Barnett Reservoir in Ridgeland and on the low ground along the Pearl River, the soil stays wet longer after rain, so that window stays open longer.

Signs a tree is at risk

  • A lean that is new, or that got worse after a wet week.
  • Soil lifting, cracking or mounding on one side of the trunk.
  • Mushrooms, or soft, spongy bark at the base — signs of decay.
  • A water oak with cavities in the trunk or a hollow sound when tapped.
  • Large dead limbs in the top of a pine.
  • Any pine within falling distance of the house, a pool or a power drop.

Any one of these is worth a look before the next storm rather than after it.

What can you do about it?

You can't change the soil, but you can change what's standing in it. A leaning pine can be taken down in sections before it chooses its own moment. An oak with decay can be assessed and removed while it's still safe to climb. Healthy trees close to the house can be pruned to reduce the sail the wind pushes against.
